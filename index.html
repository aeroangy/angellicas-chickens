Good to Know

Egg Care & Where These Eggs Come From

Fresh backyard eggs are a little different from what you find at the store — in the best possible ways. Here's what you should know.

🌸
The Bloom — Nature's Seal

When a hen lays an egg, she coats it in a natural protective layer called the bloom (or cuticle). This invisible barrier seals the pores of the shell, keeping bacteria out and moisture in — essentially nature's own food preservation system.

As long as an egg's bloom is intact and the egg is unwashed, it does not need refrigeration and can safely sit at room temperature for several weeks. This is standard practice across most of Europe and is how eggs were stored for centuries before refrigeration existed.

💡 Once you wash an egg, the bloom is removed and refrigeration becomes necessary to keep it safe. Wash just before use for best results.
🧼
How to Clean Your Eggs

If your egg has a bit of dirt or a feather on it, there's a right way to clean it without stripping the bloom unnecessarily.

Dry cleaning first: For light soiling, a dry cloth or soft brush is all you need. This removes debris without disturbing the bloom at all.

If washing is needed: Use warm water (warmer than the egg — never cold, as cold water causes the egg to contract and can draw bacteria inward through the pores). A gentle rinse is all that's required. Avoid soaking, and skip the dish soap unless the egg is heavily soiled.

💡 After washing, dry thoroughly and move to the refrigerator. Washed eggs keep well in the fridge for 4–6 weeks.
⏱️
26 Hours in the Making

Every egg is the result of a 24–26 hour journey inside the hen. From the moment a yolk is released, the body builds around it layer by layer — egg white, two shell membranes, and finally the shell itself, which takes up to 20 hours to fully mineralize with calcium. The bloom is applied as a finishing coat just before laying.

Once laid, the process starts all over again almost immediately. Our girls have no rooster since Sweet Pea moved on before the hens arrived, so every egg is purely the product of the hen's own biology, start to finish.

💡 Shell color is deposited in that final stage — blue and green shells are pigmented like a dye all the way through; brown shells are coated only on the outside. Same egg, different finish.

🐓 Snow tries her best every spring to hatch eggs through sheer determination — but without a rooster, she's sitting on unfertilized eggs. We admire her commitment.
❄️
To Refrigerate or Not?

Unwashed eggs with their bloom intact can safely sit on the counter at room temperature for up to 2–3 weeks. They need no special treatment and are perfectly safe to eat.

That said, refrigerating them extends their freshness significantly — a refrigerated egg stays at peak quality for up to 3 months. If you don't plan to use them within a week or two, the fridge is the smarter choice.

One thing to keep in mind: once an egg has been refrigerated, it's best to keep it cold. The temperature fluctuation from fridge to counter and back can cause condensation, which can compromise the bloom and invite bacteria.

💡 Our rule of thumb: counter is fine for a week, fridge if you're not sure when you'll use them.
🌾

What Our Hens Eat

Our girls are fed a fully certified organic feed — no pesticides, no GMOs, no unnecessary additives. We believe what goes into the hen goes into the egg, and we want both to be as wholesome as possible.

Beyond their feed, the hens spend their days doing what chickens do best: foraging. Our backyard provides a steady supply of bugs, beetles, worms, and whatever else they can scratch up from the soil — natural protein and enrichment that keeps them active, healthy, and entertained. A hen that forages is a happy hen, and happy hens lay better eggs.

Shell formation demands a lot of calcium, so we supplement with crushed oyster shells available free-choice, and we dry and crush the spent eggshells right back into the feeding rotation — a tidy closed loop that puts the calcium from one egg toward building the next.

We also feed a small group of outdoor cats — and the chickens have unanimously decided that cat food is a perfectly acceptable second breakfast. We have accepted this.

The Backstory

How It All Started

A spring morning, a long line, and a chicken shortage.

Mira and Dottie came home in the spring of 2025, just one day old — tiny, peeping fluffballs from Reber Ranch, just down the road in our area. Getting them wasn't easy: the avian flu was spreading across the country, chicks were scarce, and we stood in line at store opening to secure our spot.

They were raised alongside their brother, Sweet Pea — handled daily, hand-fed, and thoroughly spoiled into the affectionate hens they are today. Mira never forgot those early days of human contact.

Luna & Snow join the family.

Luna and Snow arrived from Red Dog Ranch in Oregon at 7–10 months of age, already full-grown and full of character. Part of a thoughtful Americauna-mixed breeding program designed to produce beautiful birds with striking egg colors, they brought their own dynamic to the yard. The flock found its rhythm — and its pecking order — in time.

🐓 A note about Sweet Pea

Every flock has its stories, and ours includes a handsome rooster named Sweet Pea who joined us from Graham, WA. He was charming, beautiful, and completely devoted to his girls — but he was also exceptionally loud. Our neighbors appreciated us considerably more after we found Sweet Pea a proper farm home with plenty of hens to keep him busy. We miss him. A little.
